What you've already tried

The rules don't cover the worst case scenario

Most parents arrive having done the sensible things: delayed the phone, installed the parental controls, explained the dangers that lurk online. Then they adjusted the number of hours, looked for a better blocking app, and re-negotiated the same rule again every Tuesday.

All of that is amazing and helpful and I wouldn't tell anyone to stop. But every one of those decisions is a rule about access, and a rule about access only governs what happens while you're watching.

Things go wrong in the hours when nobody is watching.

Almost nobody prepares for what the child will do once they're left to their own devices.

The proof

Why we teach it this way

We teach the thinking tools directly.

We go beyond play-based exposure and teach the thinking tools directly: pausing, questioning, evaluating evidence, considering alternatives, and explaining a conclusion.

→ Abrami, P.C. et al. (2008) — meta-analysis on explicit instruction and critical thinking, referenced via parentingscience.com

We start with stories they can actually relate to.

We start every session with a real story a child can recognize before naming the concept behind it.

This mirrors how Finland, the country ranked #1 in Europe for resilience to misinformation, sequences media-literacy learning for young people.

→ Finnish National Curriculum media literacy policy (2013, updated 2019) — via EAVI.eu and Finland Toolbox

We make kids reason out loud together.

Groups reasoning out loud together, then having to defend their conclusion, is a recognized way to build reasoning skill, used internationally by Philosophy for Children.

→ Systematic literature review, critical thinking in preschool classrooms (ScienceDirect, 2015–2021 studies)
→ Lipman, M. — Philosophy for Children / Harry Stottlemeier's Discovery

Questions parents ask

What if we miss a session?

The groups are flexible. Your child can attend any 2 sessions held for their age group throughout the month.

Is this therapy?

No. It's skills practice: regulation, influence detection and rehearsing decisions. It isn't treatment and isn't a substitute for treatment.

My child is shy. Will they have to perform?

No child is put on the spot. Quiet kids usually start by watching the role play and saying what they noticed. Speaking up comes later, once the group feels safe to them.

Can I take part?

Once a semester, in the communication workshop, together with your child. The rest of the sessions belong to the kids, and the parent community is where you and I stay in contact.

Can we try one month first?

No, six months is the minimum. Kids only start talking honestly once the group has stopped changing shape every few weeks.

What do we need at home?

A laptop or tablet with a camera, and a room where your child can talk without the rest of the family chiming in.

Keeping the group safe

Decisions School is a group environment, so every child needs to be able to participate in a way that is safe and respectful for themselves and the other kids.

Before enrolment, I speak with every parent to make sure the group is an appropriate fit. If a situation comes up where a child is unable to participate safely or appropriately, I'll speak with the parent first and, where possible, work with them on a way forward.

If the child continues to be unable to participate safely or appropriately in the group, I may ask the family to leave the programme. This isn't about punishment or getting a child in trouble. It's about protecting the group's ability to be a place where kids can speak, disagree, practise, and make mistakes without feeling unsafe.

I'll handle these situations directly with the parent rather than discussing them with the group.

How I teach

How I know these skills can be taught

I've taught children before, and some of my favourite moments happened as they took something we'd practised and started using it somewhere I hadn't taught them to use it.

Once, I showed my kindergarten class what happens when you put a pen in a glass of water: the way light refracts can make the pen look bent or displaced.

I explained the ways sun-rays can either do that or reflect like a mirror.

A while later, we were reading a story about a bunny shocked at what it was seeing in a lake.

One of the kids, K., stopped and said it was the bunny's image in the water. Then he explained why: the rays and how light can behave in such a way.

He was 6.

He recognized the pattern himself and used it to make sense of something new.

The final year of kindergarten didn't include afternoon naps, and the kids were often exhausted by the afternoon.

So I taught them some simple breathing and meditation exercises to help regulate themselves.

D. had some health issues that sometimes made his ears hurt.

One day, he started using the breathing technique by himself when his ears hurt.
With zero prompting.

He remembered a tool we'd practiced together and reached for it when he needed it.

That's the part that matters to me: when the adult isn't there to supply the answer, the child still has something to reach for.
